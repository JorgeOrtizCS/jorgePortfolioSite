A couple weekends ago I went to SFISSA's Hack the Flag and Chili Cookoff. It was my first CTF
ever. My team took first place. It should feel like an unqualified win, and in a lot of ways it
was, but the honest version of the story is messier than "we won," and I think the messy version
is more useful to write down.

## The win

Our team cleared the board and finished in first. Great day, great people, and it was genuinely
cool to compete alongside people who have been doing this a lot longer than I have. If you just
look at the scoreboard, it looks like I belonged there.

## The part that is not on the scoreboard

Here is what actually happened on most of those challenges: I watched. We had an AI assistant
helping the team, and across most of the board it was doing the technical work while I followed
along instead of driving. The categories that gave us trouble were not things I could reason
through on my own yet, so I leaned on the tool instead of the skill.

OSINT was the place I felt most useful. Tracking a subject across social media profiles, pulling
metadata out of photos, piecing together where someone had been. That is patience and attention
to detail more than deep technical knowledge, and it is the one part of the weekend where I felt
like a contributor rather than a spectator.

I could leave that paragraph out. Nobody at the event is going to publish a breakdown of who
solved what, and the scoreboard says first place either way. I am putting it in because a result
I cannot reproduce is not worth much to me, and pretending otherwise mostly just delays finding
that out.

## What I actually got out of it

The challenge I actually drove myself was a crypto one called procrastinator. A Flask app with a
`.git` directory left in the shipped source, a secret key hardcoded in the first commit and
"removed" in the second, and a session cookie I could forge once I had that key. I typed every
command in that one, including all the wrong ones, and I wrote it up properly
[here](/blog/006-procrastinator-git-history).

The other one I spent real time on was an OSINT scenario, working through a case file to decide
whether a student was actually a threat. The answer turned out to be that the evidence had been
cooked, and catching that was reading comprehension more than tooling. That is the kind of work I
am good at right now.

That is not the same as being able to do it cold, and I want to be clear that I have not done
that yet. Working through something with guidance and then doing it unassisted are different
skills, and only the second one is the one employers are actually buying. Knowing precisely where
that line sits for me right now is the most useful thing I took home from the weekend.

## What I am doing about it

The plan is not complicated. Redo that forensics case start to finish with no assistance and no
walkthrough, and write up whatever breaks. Keep grinding the offensive labs I have been working
through, where I am forced to drive because there is nobody else on the keyboard. Build the
fundamentals in the categories I had to sit out, so that next year the scoreboard and my actual
contribution are the same number.

A win does not automatically mean competence, and the gap between the two is exactly where the
next few months of work need to go. I am not treating "I do not know enough" as a source of
anxiety anymore, I am using it as a checklist. OSINT is a lane I can build on. Everything else
just tells me where to look next.

On to the next one.
